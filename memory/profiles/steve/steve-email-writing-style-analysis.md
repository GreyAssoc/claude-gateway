# Steve Whitehead Email Writing Style Analysis

**Analysis Period:** Last 90 days (November 2024 - February 2026)
**Email Address:** steve.w@greyandassociates.co.uk
**Analysis Date:** February 26, 2026

---

## Executive Summary

Steve Whitehead's email writing style is characterized by **professional brevity, technical clarity, and practical directness**. His communications are task-oriented, often instructional, and maintain a consistently polite but efficient tone. His emails focus on delivering actionable information with minimal embellishment.

---

## 1. Tone and Formality Level

### **Professional but Approachable**
- **Medium-High Formality:** Maintains professional standards while being personable
- **Polite without being overly formal:** Uses standard courtesies without elaborate pleasantries
- **Confident and authoritative:** Demonstrates expertise through clear directives

### Examples:
- **Opening:** "Hi Teodora," / "Dear Pedro" / "Hi Mick,"
- **Closing:** "Kind regards," (most common)
- **Authority statements:** "I have to advise you that..." / "I would recommend that..."

---

## 2. Sentence Structure and Length

### **Characteristics:**
- **Short to medium sentences:** Typically 10-25 words
- **Direct structure:** Subject-verb-object with minimal subordinate clauses
- **Lists and bullet points:** Used for clarity when presenting multiple items
- **Paragraph breaks:** Frequent use of single-line paragraphs for emphasis

### Examples:

**Short, direct:**
> "Thank you for getting in touch."

**Medium complexity:**
> "Will the party wall with #11 need underpinning to create the lower ground floor extension?"

**Instructional:**
> "Please send through your plans (existing, proposed & structural if you have them) and, once received, I'll be able to provide you with a quote."

## 5. Use of Punctuation

### **Patterns:**
- **Periods:** Standard use at end of complete sentences
- **Question marks:** Used appropriately for direct questions
- **Exclamation marks:** Rare; used occasionally for enthusiasm ("Good luck with your build!")
- **Colons:** Used for introducing instructions or information
- **Semicolons:** Minimal use
- **Dashes/Hyphens:** Standard use in compound words
- **Ampersands (&):** Used in company name and occasionally in lists
- **Bold/Asterisks:** Used for emphasis in signature (*T:*, *W:*, *Steve Whitehead*)

### Examples:
- Technical lists: "existing, proposed & structural"
- Contact info: "T: 020 8150 3762"
- Emphasis in important items: "*Please ensure you issue the policies for Security for Expenses(1 & 3 St Lukes Street) before works start.*"

## 6. Paragraph Structure

### **Characteristics:**
- **Single-line paragraphs:** Very common for individual points
- **Double line breaks:** Used between distinct thoughts or sections
- **Short paragraphs:** Typically 1-3 sentences maximum
- **Visual hierarchy:** Uses spacing to create scannable structure

### Example Pattern:
```
Hi [Name],

[Opening pleasantry]

[Main point/request - often single line]

[Supporting detail or instruction]

[Follow-up instruction]

[Closing pleasantry if applicable]


Kind regards,

[Signature]
```

## 7. Level of Detail Provided

### **Characteristics:**
- **Precise technical requirements:** When discussing work, Steve is specific
- **Contextual information:** Provides necessary background
- **Legal/procedural clarity:** Especially clear about timelines and obligations
- **Practical next steps:** Always includes actionable items

### Examples:

**High detail when needed:**
> "I have to advise you that you have 14 days in which to appeal the Award in the County Court if you consider there may be something wrong but I also would advise that I believe it is properly constituted in accordance with the Act and there are no grounds to make such an appeal."

**Sufficient detail for technical work:**
> "I'm working on the notice now and noticed that the foundation for the steel box frame (see below) goes under the existing party wall on both sides which means the party wall is to be underpinning, however in plan view it shows the underpinning extends under only half the thickness of the party wall."

**Clear technical requests:**
> "Will the party wall with #11 need underpinning to create the lower ground floor extension? If so, I will need a section through the underpinning with the depth of excavation/underpinning explicitly stated in order to serve notice."

## 8. Use of Formatting

### **Bold:**
- Used in signature: **Steve Whitehead** MFPWS MPTS
- Used for labels: **T:**, **W:**
- Used for emphasis in body (occasionally): **before**
- Company name: **Grey & Associates Ltd**

### **Underline:**
- Used sparingly for critical emphasis: "*Please ensure you issue the policies for Security for Expenses(1 & 3 St Lukes Street) before works start.*"

### **Capitalization:**
- Standard sentence case
- Proper nouns correctly capitalized
- Company abbreviations: MFPWS, MPTS

### **Lists:**
- Rarely uses bullet points in body
- Uses line breaks instead for list-like information
- Sometimes uses numbered items for multiple points

### **Attachments:**
- References attachments explicitly: "Please find attached..."
- Names specific documents: "Also enclosed is our invoice..."

## 9. Technical vs. Plain Language

### **Balance:**
Steve strikes an effective balance between technical precision and accessibility:

**Technical Language (when appropriate):**
- "duly served on you in accordance with the Party Wall etc. Act 1996"
- "eccentric to the steel columns"
- "underpinning"
- "excavation/underpinning"
- "party wall surveyor"
- "Award"
- "Section 3", "Section 6"

**Plain Language (for clarity):**
- "I'm working on the notice now"
- "Thank you for getting in touch"
- "Good luck with your build!"
- "Please send through your plans"
- "once received, I'll be able to..."

**Translation approach:**
When technical terms are necessary, Steve provides context rather than jargon-dumping.

## 10. Politeness Markers and Softeners

### **Softening Language:**
- "I would recommend that..." (vs. "You must...")
- "Please ensure..." (vs. "You need to...")
- "If you could..." (vs. "You should...")
- "I would appreciate it" (vs. "Do this")
- "at your earliest convenience" (vs. "immediately")

### **Courtesy Markers:**
- "Please" - Used frequently but not excessively
- "Thank you" - Standard acknowledgment
- "Kind regards" - Consistent sign-off
- "I hope you're keeping well" - Personal touch in openings

### **Authority with Politeness:**
Steve maintains authority while being polite:
> "I have to advise you that..." (obligation framed politely)
> "Please ensure you contact us once building works are complete so that we can revisit..."

---

## 11. Directness vs. Indirectness

### **Predominantly Direct:**
Steve favors directness but softens it with politeness markers.

**Direct requests:**
- "Please send through your plans"
- "Please also send through the full set of structural drawings"
- "Please ask your engineer to amend the design"

**Direct statements:**
- "I'm working on the notice now"
- "I will need a section through the underpinning"
- "Once the amended drawings are received I'll be able to serve notice"

**Softened directives:**
- "I would recommend that the new foundation under the frame is eccentric to the steel columns so that the party wall doesn't need to be underpinned."
- "If you could settle both invoices and the invoice you received earlier today from the checking engineer at your earliest convenience I would appreciate it."

### **Conditional Phrasing:**
Uses "if/when" clauses to soften demands:
- "If so, I will need..."
- "Once received, I'll be able to..."
- "If you could settle... I would appreciate it"

## 14. Context-Specific Variations

### **To Clients (Building Owners):**
- **More formal tone**
- **More explanatory**
- **Includes legal advisories**
- **More courteous language**
- **Detailed next steps**

Example:
> "Dear Pedro, Please find attached the Award, which is duly served on you in accordance with the Party Wall etc. Act 1996..."

### **To Architects/Engineers:**
- **Technical and specific**
- **Direct requests for information**
- **Assumes shared technical knowledge**

Example:
> "Hi Mick, Will the party wall with #11 need underpinning to create the lower ground floor extension? If so, I will need a section through the underpinning with the depth of excavation/underpinning explicitly stated in order to serve notice."

### **To Neighbors:**
- **Clear and informative**
- **Patient tone**
- **Explanation-focused**

Example:
> "Dear Hossein & Sima, I hope you're keeping well. Kyoko (copied in) has been in touch to let me know that the new wall excavations must go where a rose bush is located in your garden..."

### **Internal Team Communications:**
- **Extremely brief**
- **Action-oriented**
- **Shorthand acceptable ("Pls")**
- **Subject-line style messages**

Example:
> "Pls pdf, print & post notices for Adela Ave job."

## 18. Distinctive Writing Habits

### **Never Uses:**
- Emojis
- Excessive punctuation (!!!, ???)
- All caps (except acronyms)
- Informal greetings (Hey, Hi there)
- Slang or colloquialisms
- Contractions in formal contexts (minimal use overall)

### **Always Uses:**
- Full signature block
- "Kind regards," as closing
- Professional credentials after name
- Clear subject lines
- Proper paragraph spacing
- References to attachments when applicable

### **Occasionally Uses:**
- Parenthetical clarifications: "(existing, proposed & structural if you have them)"
- Conditional phrasing: "if you could..."
- Emphasis through formatting: bold, underline
- Well-wishes: "Good luck with your build!"

## 19. Communication Efficiency

### **Time-Saving Techniques:**
- **Single-issue focus:** One main point per email when possible
- **Clear requests:** No ambiguity about what's needed
- **Conditional phrasing:** "Once received, I'll be able to..." sets expectations
- **Reference by location:** "The party wall with #11" rather than lengthy descriptions
- **Template efficiency:** Similar structure allows quick composition

### **Recipient Clarity:**
- **Explicit next steps:** Always clear what recipient should do
- **Timeline indicators:** "14 days", "at your earliest convenience", "once received"
- **Copy appropriate parties:** Includes relevant people (architect, other surveyor, team)
- **Document references:** Names specific attachments or documents

## 20. Summary of Key Style Elements

### **Top 10 Defining Characteristics:**

1. **Professional brevity** - Says what needs to be said, nothing more
2. **Consistent structure** - Reliable formatting across all emails
3. **Clear action items** - Always evident what recipient should do
4. **Polite directness** - Direct requests softened with courtesy markers
5. **Technical precision** - Accurate terminology when required
6. **Accessible language** - Technical terms balanced with clarity
7. **Visual spacing** - Uses line breaks for readability
8. **Standard signature** - Never varies professional sign-off
9. **Context-appropriate tone** - Adjusts formality for audience
10. **Efficient phrasing** - Gets to the point quickly

## Style Guide for Emulating Steve's Writing

### **DO:**
- ✓ Start with "Hi [Name]," or "Dear [Name]," based on relationship formality
- ✓ Keep opening pleasantries brief (one line maximum)
- ✓ Use single-line paragraphs for distinct points
- ✓ End with "Kind regards,"
- ✓ Include full signature block with credentials
- ✓ Use "Please" before requests
- ✓ State exactly what you need
- ✓ Use conditional phrasing: "If you could...", "Once received..."
- ✓ Be specific about documents and technical requirements
- ✓ Include relevant parties on CC
- ✓ Reference attachments explicitly
- ✓ Provide clear next steps
- ✓ Use double line breaks between sections

### **DON'T:**
- ✗ Use emojis or excessive punctuation
- ✗ Write long, complex sentences
- ✗ Include unnecessary background
- ✗ Use informal language unless internal team email
- ✗ Omit clear action items
- ✗ Forget to mention attachments if present
- ✗ Use overly casual greetings
- ✗ Write long paragraphs (3+ sentences)
- ✗ Use vague timelines ("soon", "later")
- ✗ Forget professional credentials in signature
- ✗ Vary signature format
- ✗ Use contractions in formal correspondence
